How to Remove Shadows from a Scan and Sharpen It

Updated November 2026 · about 6 minutes to read

Shadow on a scan is the most common reason a document looks unprofessional: a dark band down one side, a stripe across the middle, or a page that is uniformly grey. The good news is that most shadows are uneven illumination, and algorithms can flatten them. The bad news is that once a shadow has crushed the text to solid black, no filter can bring it back. This guide covers where shadows come from, how to avoid them while shooting, and the correct order to apply enhancement filters.

Where scan shadows come from

Brightness in a photo is set by the light source, and a light source is almost never perfectly even:

  • Self-shadowing — your hand or phone blocks light from one side, producing a diagonal dark band. This is the most common cause.
  • A point light too close — a desk lamp near the page gives a bright centre with falloff towards the edges.
  • A page that is not flat — the curve of a book spine is brighter at the crest and darker in the valley.
  • Paper that is off-white — old documents and recycled stock simply are not pure white.
  • Auto-exposure biased dark — a bright background makes the meter underexpose the sheet.

The first three are uneven illumination and can be normalised by software. The last two are an overall tint or underexposure, which needs brightening and a redefined white point.

Stage one: kill shadows at the source

  1. Light from both sides. Avoid a single harsh source; diffused daylight near a window is close to ideal and produces almost no hard shadow.
  2. Do not block the light. Stand to one side so neither your body nor the phone sits between the source and the page.
  3. Skip the flash. A flash is a very close point source: it blows out the centre and falls off sharply.
  4. Flatten the page. Weight the far edge with a book or press it down (keeping your fingers out of frame) to reduce ripples.
  5. Lock the exposure. Tap the paper in your camera app and pull the exposure compensation down so the background stops fooling the meter.

Stage two: apply filters in the right order

Step 1 — flatten illumination with colour enhancement

An enhancement filter such as Magic Color normalises illumination separately for the red, green and blue channels: it estimates a lighting map from the local background and divides each pixel by it. The paper snaps back to near-white while ink and stamp colours survive. This is the step that removes diagonal bands and dark edges.

Step 2 — choose the final look for the content

Step 3 — check for broken strokes and noise

Black & white works by forcing everything past a brightness threshold to pure white. Set the threshold aggressively and thin strokes break, decimal points vanish, and punctuation merges. When that happens, fall back to greyscale or colour enhancement — usually faster than tweaking the threshold further.

Frequently asked questions

Can shadows on a scan be removed completely?

Mostly, yes. An enhancement filter that normalises illumination per colour channel flattens uneven lighting and pushes the paper back to near-white. If a shadow crushed the text to solid black, no filter can recover the detail and the page must be re-shot.

Should I use black and white or colour enhancement to remove shadows?

Black and white gives the sharpest, smallest result for text-only pages. Use colour enhancement when there are stamps, signatures, highlights or background patterns, because it removes the shadow while keeping colour.

Why is my whole scan grey?

Usually the shot was underexposed or the paper itself is off-white. Use the brighten filter to lift exposure, then apply greyscale or black and white to push the background to pure white.

The text became thin or broken after removing shadows. What now?

That means the filter threshold was too aggressive. Switch from black and white to greyscale or colour enhancement, which keeps strokes intact while still whitening the paper.
